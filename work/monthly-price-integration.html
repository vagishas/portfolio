Overview

Apple Card Monthly Installments (ACMI) lets people pay for Apple products monthly with Apple Card, interest-free. It launched on iPhone. Our team's job was to bring it into the Apple.com buy flow for more products, from the product page to the bag to checkout.

By the end, financing wasn't a payment option people stumbled on at checkout. It was part of how price was shown across the whole flow.

The project was broad, with dozens of screens and states. Rather than walk through all of it, this case study focuses on the decisions that shaped the experience, and the messy cases that tested it hardest.

The challenge

Apple Card is a credit card, and credit cards come with terms and conditions that aren't easy to digest. The challenge was to keep people fully informed without burying them in jargon, and to make applying for a card feel as simple as the rest of Apple's buy flow.

  • Every word carried weightLegal and financial wording near a price couldn't simply be cut.
  • Very little spaceOn a phone, price, terms and trade-in details all had to fit in a small area.
  • Prices that changeConfiguration, trade-in credit and payment terms all changed the monthly price.
  • Two kinds of customerThe same flow had to work for existing cardholders and people hearing about Apple Card for the first time.

Making Apple Card visible, without overpowering

Apple Card was still new, so people needed a reason to learn more. We explored where the Apple Card message should live and how loud it should be. Some versions were easy to miss. Others overpowered the product information, or blended into it so people couldn't tell product details from financing details.

We placed a short banner and low monthly prices at the moments people were already making decisions: choosing a configuration, and reviewing their bag. In testing, people clicked a link that called out 0% APR far more often than a plain "Learn more", so the copy led with that value.

Showing two prices at once

The bag is where people check what they're paying for: products, taxes, shipping, trade-in credit. Adding a second way to pay could easily make it confusing.

We explored several ways to show pricing and tested them with users. In the final design, the bag total shows both the monthly and the full price side by side at the bottom of the page, so people can compare their options right where they decide how to pay.

The button wording mattered as much as the layout. In testing, people hesitated to click anything close to "Apply", because they worried about a hit to their credit score. "Check Out with Apple Card Monthly Installments" felt safer, because it set the right expectation: they'd see the details before committing.

Explaining the terms in plain language

With full-price and monthly items in the same bag, there wasn't room to explain everything inline. So choosing Apple Card Monthly Installments opens a payment details modal.

It's a modal rather than a new page on purpose: it feels like an extension of the bag, so people understand their payments before they commit. Working with our copywriters, I focused the content on two distinctions people cared about most: full price vs. monthly price, and what's due today vs. the total over time.

Designing for the messy cases

The happy path, one iPhone paid monthly, was the easy part. The system only worked if it held up for real carts, and those were the cases I owned:

  • Mixed bags. Some items could be paid monthly and some couldn't. The bag total still had to make sense at a glance.
  • Different installment terms. Products could have different terms, for example 12 months and 6 months, in the same order.
  • Trade-in credit. A trade-in lowers the amount being financed, so every monthly price had to reflect it.

Keeping information close, on purpose

One usability finding was that the Apple Card information felt repetitive. We made a conscious decision to keep it. People went back and forth between product details and pricing before deciding, and many nuances of a credit product are easy to overlook. Keeping the key details and links close at hand at each step supported that back-and-forth instead of fighting it.

Outcome

By the end of the project, Apple Card Monthly Installments had become part of how price was communicated. Monthly pricing appeared alongside full price wherever price appeared. A merchandising banner in the bag and checkout led people to it, and a clear payment choice at checkout let them commit without leaving the flow.

  • Scale: shipped across most of Apple's online storefronts.
  • Longevity: stayed live until Apple.com's next redesign, and parts of the approach carried into the new design.

I moved on before post-launch data was available, so I can't share numbers.

What I learned

  • People are wary of credit. Past experiences with hidden fees and high interest make people cautious, so every button has to say exactly what happens next.
  • Decisions aren't linear. People move back and forth to compare, so each detail needs to be purposeful and easy to find again.
  • The total matters most. People want to know how much they'll pay in total, and they decide based on that.
